    From headline value to realizable value

    AI almost always does a task faster than a person. But someone must check the work, redo what fails, and absorb the cost of errors that slip through. Counting those costs, about a fifth of the headline value of AI-exposed work is realizable today.

    Value of AI-exposed work hours, U.S. economy, billions of dollars. Base case: 90% of failures caught; an error that escapes costs 5 times the task's value times its consequence.

    Why AI fluency is an economic measure

    If reviewers check AI work 25% faster and let 25% fewer errors through, realizable value rises from $649B to $848B.

    Updates

    A living estimate, updated every year

    AI is changing faster than the data that measure it. We treat these results as a starting point and will re-run the full model once a year as new federal data and new evidence on AI use come out, so the picture sharpens over time.

    What this edition shows. The 2026 edition rests on data from before AI agents were widely used: employment and wages through May 2025, and Census age data for 2019 and 2024. It captures the effect of generative AI, which drafts and answers. It does not yet capture agentic AI, which takes actions on its own.
    Waiting for 2025 data. The Census Bureau's 2025 American Community Survey microdata, the first year in which AI agents were in wide use, have no release date yet. When they come out, we will run our pre-registered test of whether agentic AI is thinning out entry-level hiring, and fold the result into the next edition.
    Every year. Each edition adds the latest federal wage and employment data, the newest O*NET task data and the latest measures of how people actually use AI at work. We also revisit the scenarios and assumptions behind the ranges.
    Known gaps we plan to close. The model does not yet cover robots and other physical AI. It reads the 2035 scenario nationally, so state results reflect each state's mix of occupations, not local adoption rates.
